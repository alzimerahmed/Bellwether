Our Story

We started with one machine, a rented kitchen and a fairly unrealistic view of how much ice cream two people can sell in a weekend.

01 The premise

Small On Purpose

Two gallons is a small batch by any commercial standard, and it is not the efficient way to do this. It is the batch size where one person can taste everything that goes out, adjust the salt in the next run, and pull something that isn't right before it reaches a counter.

The trade-off is that we run out. A flavor that does unusually well on a Saturday might not be back until Tuesday. We have decided we would rather explain that than fix it by making more at once.

02 How we got here

How We Got Here

Year one

One machine in a rented commercial kitchen, four flavors, and a folding table at the Saturday market. We sold out by eleven most weeks, which we misread as a good sign rather than a capacity problem.

Year two

Took over the Old Mill space, mostly because it had drainage and a loading door. Put in a proper churn and a counter, and started making the dairy-free line on its own equipment after enough people asked.

Year four

Opened Riverside. Learned that running two locations from one kitchen means the second one gets whatever the first did not sell, and rebuilt the morning delivery run around that.

Last spring

Harbor Green, deliberately small — a counter and a freezer, no seating. Three shops is as far as one kitchen goes, so the next one means a second kitchen.

We would rather explain why we ran out than fix it by making more at once.

03 How we churn

The Actual Process

01

Steep first

Vanilla pods, mint leaves and coffee all go into the cream overnight rather than as extract at the end. It takes a day longer and tastes like the actual ingredient instead of a flavoring of it.

02

Churn slow, churn cold

Slower churning works less air into the base. The result is denser and melts more evenly, which is most of why a small-batch scoop feels different in the mouth.

03

Fold in by hand

Ripples and mix-ins go in at the end with a paddle, not a machine, so they stay in streaks and shards instead of being distributed into a uniform beige.

04 Where it comes from

Where It Comes From

Dairy arrives twice a week from two farms close enough that we have visited both more than once. Fruit is seasonal and local where it can be, which is the real reason the seasonal list moves around instead of a marketing decision.

Cocoa, vanilla and pistachios cannot be local, so those are bought on quality and from importers who will tell us where a lot came from.
